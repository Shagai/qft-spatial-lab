<h2 id="question">Why replace the scattering angle by angular momentum?</h2>

In the center-of-momentum frame, two spinless incoming particles approach with momenta $\mathbf k$ and $-\mathbf k$. After an elastic collision their momentum magnitudes remain $k$, while the outgoing direction changes. The amplitude is therefore a function of the angle

$$
\cos\theta
=
\widehat{\mathbf k}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf k'}.
$$

One could study that angular function directly. Rotational symmetry suggests a better basis. Expand it into states of definite orbital angular momentum $\ell$. Each value of $\ell$ is a separate way for the incoming wave to meet the interaction.

The analogy with Fourier analysis is exact in spirit:

$$
\boxed{
\text{angle space}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{angular-momentum channels}.
}
$$

A complicated angular pattern may require many partial waves, but rotational invariance prevents different total angular momenta from mixing. The $S$-matrix becomes block diagonal, and unitarity can be applied one finite block at a time.

<h2 id="expansion">The spinless partial-wave expansion</h2>

For elastic scattering of distinguishable spinless particles, choose the normalization

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2.
$$

The rotational basis on the sphere is supplied by Legendre polynomials:

$$
\boxed{
f(\theta)
=
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
(2\ell+1)f_\ell(k)P_\ell(\cos\theta).
}
$$

$f_\ell$ has dimensions of length. It is often cleaner to introduce the dimensionless partial-wave amplitude

$$
a_\ell(k)=k f_\ell(k).
$$

Orthogonality lets us recover an individual channel:

$$
f_\ell(k)
=
\frac12\int_{-1}^{1}
d(\cos\theta)\,
P_\ell(\cos\theta)f(\theta).
$$

The low values have a geometric interpretation. The $s$ wave, $\ell=0$, is isotropic. A $p$ wave, $\ell=1$, has one unit of orbital angular momentum and a simple forward-backward angular dependence. Higher partial waves carry increasingly fine angular structure.

<Sidenote>
For identical particles, the amplitude must also be symmetrized or antisymmetrized. This removes the inappropriate odd or even partial waves rather than changing the unitarity argument itself.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="channel-s-matrix">One complex number for each elastic channel</h2>

At fixed $k$, the $S$-matrix eigenvalue in a spinless partial wave is related to $a_\ell$ by

$$
\boxed{S_\ell=1+2ia_\ell}
$$

or equivalently

$$
f_\ell
=
\frac{S_\ell-1}{2ik}.
$$

If the only possible outcome is elastic scattering back into the same channel, unitarity requires

$$
|S_\ell|=1.
$$

Every complex number of unit magnitude is a phase, so write

$$
\boxed{S_\ell=e^{2i\delta_\ell}.}
$$

$\delta_\ell(k)$ is the <Term>phase shift</Term>. The factor of two is conventional and useful. It gives

$$
a_\ell
=
e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell
$$

and

$$
f_\ell
=
\frac{e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell}{k}.
$$

The interaction has changed the phase of the outgoing component relative to free propagation. That phase change alone encodes the entire elastic response of this angular-momentum channel.

When other channels are open, use

$$
\boxed{
S_\ell
=
\eta_\ell e^{2i\delta_\ell},
\qquad
0\leq\eta_\ell\leq1.
}
$$

$\eta_\ell$ is the <Term>inelasticity</Term>. The phase shift $\delta_\ell$ remains real. $\eta_\ell=1$ means no probability leaves the elastic channel, while $\eta_\ell<1$ measures leakage into other final states with the same conserved quantum numbers.

<h2 id="unitarity-circle">Elastic unitarity is a circle</h2>

For a purely elastic channel,

$$
a_\ell=e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell.
$$

Its real and imaginary parts satisfy

$$
\boxed{
(\operatorname{Re}a_\ell)^2
+
\left(\operatorname{Im}a_\ell-\frac12\right)^2
=
\frac14.
}
$$

As the energy changes, the amplitude moves around a circle centered at $i/2$ with radius $1/2$. Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{Im}a_\ell=|a_\ell|^2.
$$

That is the optical theorem resolved channel by channel.

If inelastic states are available, the elastic amplitude obeys

$$
\operatorname{Im}a_\ell
=
|a_\ell|^2
+I_\ell,
\qquad
I_\ell\geq0,
$$

$I_\ell$ collects the positive contribution of the inelastic channels. The corresponding point lies inside the elastic circle. The full coupled-channel matrix remains unitary; only the one elastic entry has lost magnitude.

<h2 id="cross-sections">Each channel keeps its own probability ledger</h2>

Orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials eliminates interference between different $\ell$ values after integrating over all angles. The elastic cross-section is

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}
=
\frac{\pi}{k^2}
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
(2\ell+1)|1-S_\ell|^2.
}
$$

The cross-section for leaving the elastic channel is

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_{\mathrm{reaction}}
=
\frac{\pi}{k^2}
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
(2\ell+1)(1-|S_\ell|^2).
}
$$

The optical theorem gives their sum:

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
\frac{2\pi}{k^2}
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
(2\ell+1)(1-\operatorname{Re}S_\ell).
}
$$

Indeed,

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}
+
\sigma_{\mathrm{reaction}}.
$$

For a purely elastic channel,

$$
\sigma_\ell
=
\frac{4\pi}{k^2}
(2\ell+1)\sin^2\delta_\ell.
$$

Because $\sin^2\delta_\ell\leq1$, every partial wave has a maximum:

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_\ell
\leq
\frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2\ell+1).
}
$$

The bound is saturated at $\delta_\ell=\pi/2$ modulo $\pi$. Stronger coupling cannot make that one channel arbitrarily large; unitarity has already used all of its available probability.

<h2 id="thresholds">Angular momentum predicts threshold behavior</h2>

Consider a short-range reaction with incoming relative momentum $k$, outgoing relative momentum $k'$, and orbital angular momenta $\ell$ and $\ell'$. Regularity near zero momentum gives the schematic behavior

$$
\mathcal M_{\ell'\ell}
\propto
k^{\ell}k'^{\ell'}.
$$

Two-body phase space contributes another factor $k'/k$, so

$$
\sigma_{\ell\to\ell'}
\propto
\frac{k'}{k}
k^{2\ell}k'^{2\ell'}.
$$

This one expression produces three useful regimes.

### Exothermic reaction

The outgoing momentum remains nonzero while $k\to0$:

$$
\sigma
\propto
k^{2\ell-1}.
$$

For an $s$-wave entrance channel, $\sigma\propto1/k\sim1/v$. The cross-section grows for a slow incident particle, but the rate coefficient $v\sigma$ approaches a constant.

### Endothermic reaction

The incoming momentum is finite at threshold while $k'\to0$:

$$
\sigma
\propto
k'^{2\ell'+1}.
$$

Since $k'\propto\sqrt{E-E_{\mathrm{th}}}$ for a two-body final state,

$$
\boxed{
\sigma
\propto
(E-E_{\mathrm{th}})^{\ell'+1/2}.
}
$$

An $s$-wave channel turns on as a square root. A $p$-wave channel turns on as the three-halves power because the centrifugal barrier suppresses it near threshold.

### Elastic reaction

Here $k'=k\to0$. Higher partial waves disappear rapidly, leaving the $s$ wave as the generic low-energy contribution. This is why very slow short-range scattering often becomes nearly isotropic even when the underlying interaction is not simple.

<Callout title="Where the threshold law can fail">
These powers assume a short-range interaction and an amplitude regular at zero momentum. Coulomb forces, massless exchange, or a pole extremely close to threshold can change the counting.
</Callout>

<h2 id="scattering-length">One number controls ordinary low-energy scattering</h2>

For the $s$ wave, define the scattering length $a$ through the effective-range expansion

$$
\boxed{
k\cot\delta_0(k)
=
-\frac1a
+
\frac12 r_e k^2
+
O(k^4).
}
$$

$r_e$ is the effective range. Using

$$
f_0(k)
=
\frac{1}{k\cot\delta_0-ik},
$$

we obtain

$$
f_0(k)\longrightarrow-a
$$

as $k\to0$. For distinguishable spinless particles with no competing spin channels,

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}\longrightarrow4\pi a^2.}
$$

The sign of $a$ affects the phase and the location of nearby poles even though this zero-energy cross-section depends only on $a^2$. An unusually large $|a|$ signals a shallow bound state or virtual state close to threshold. In the regime $|a|\gg1/k$, the amplitude approaches the $s$-wave unitarity limit rather than growing without bound.

<h2 id="coupled-channels">Spin turns each partial wave into a matrix</h2>

For particles with spin, orbital angular momentum is not generally conserved by itself. The conserved rotational label is total angular momentum

$$
\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S.
$$

At fixed $J$ and other exact quantum numbers, several orbital-spin combinations and several particle species may mix. The single number $S_\ell$ becomes a channel matrix

$$
S^J_{ba}(s).
$$

If every open channel with those quantum numbers is included, then

$$
(S^J)^\dagger S^J=1.
$$

Diagonalizing that matrix gives eigenchannels with eigenphases. If some many-particle channels are omitted, the displayed block is a contraction instead, and its eigenvalues can lie inside the unit circle.

For relativistic particles it is often convenient to use helicity states. Legendre polynomials are then replaced by Wigner $d$-matrices, and the partial waves carry helicity indices. The bookkeeping becomes richer, but the structure does not change: rotational symmetry isolates fixed $J$, and unitarity constrains the matrix within each $J$ block.

<h2 id="black-disk">A perfectly absorbing disk still scatters elastically</h2>

At high momentum, orbital angular momentum has the semiclassical interpretation

$$
b\simeq\frac{\ell}{k},
$$

where $b$ is the impact parameter. Imagine an interaction region of radius $R$ that completely absorbs waves with $b<R$ and leaves the others untouched:

$$
S_\ell\simeq
\begin{cases}
0, & \ell\lesssim kR,\\
1, & \ell\gtrsim kR.
\end{cases}
$$

Using

$$
\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}(2\ell+1)=(L+1)^2,
$$

the cross-sections become

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}\simeq\pi R^2,
\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{reaction}}\simeq\pi R^2,
\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}\simeq2\pi R^2.
}
$$

The inelastic area $\pi R^2$ agrees with the geometric shadow. The equal elastic contribution is a wave effect: removing the incident wave from the disk region produces diffraction around its boundary. Perfect absorption therefore does not eliminate elastic scattering.

<Sidenote>
Do not confuse black absorption, $S_\ell=0$, with saturation of the purely elastic bound, $S_\ell=-1$. They use probability differently and give different cross-sections per channel.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

The essential chain is:

1. Rotational symmetry expands a spinless amplitude in Legendre partial waves.
2. Each elastic channel has $S_\ell=1+2ia_\ell$.
3. Pure elasticity gives $S_\ell=e^{2i\delta_\ell}$ and places $a_\ell$ on the unitarity circle.
4. Open channels introduce $S_\ell=\eta_\ell e^{2i\delta_\ell}$ with $\eta_\ell<1$, moving the elastic amplitude inside the circle.
5. Elastic, reaction, and total cross-sections form an exact channel-by-channel ledger.
6. Angular momentum barriers determine how reactions turn on at threshold.
7. Low-energy short-range scattering is usually dominated by the $s$-wave scattering length.
8. With spin or several species, each fixed-$J$ partial wave becomes a unitary channel matrix.
9. The black-disk limit shows how absorption and diffraction divide the total cross-section equally.

Partial waves make scattering less mysterious by assigning every piece of probability to a definite angular-momentum channel.
