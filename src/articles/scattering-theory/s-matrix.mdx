<h2 id="question">A collision is a quantum question, not a movie</h2>

Suppose a source prepares a localized incoming configuration $|\alpha\rangle_0$. After the packets overlap and separate, a detector asks whether the outgoing configuration is $|\beta\rangle_0$. Scattering theory assigns one complex number to that question.

The number is not a probability by itself. It is a probability <em>amplitude</em>, retaining the phase information needed for interference between indistinguishable alternatives. Only after the amplitude is combined with normalized packets—or translated into flux and phase space—does it become a measured probability, rate, or cross-section.

This is why the $S$-matrix is both modest and powerful. It does not say what “really happened” at each intermediate time. It contains exactly the information needed to predict every asymptotic detector record.

<h2 id="definition">Compare the two asymptotic dictionaries</h2>

The Møller operators introduced in the previous section map the common asymptotic Fock space into the interacting scattering space:

$$
|\alpha,\mathrm{in}\rangle
=
\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}|\alpha\rangle_0,
\qquad
|\beta,\mathrm{out}\rangle
=
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}|\beta\rangle_0.
$$

Their comparison defines the scattering operator:

$$
\boxed{
S
=
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}^\dagger
\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}.
}
$$

Its matrix elements are

$$
\boxed{
S_{\beta\alpha}
=
{}_0\langle\beta|S|\alpha\rangle_0
=
\langle\beta,\mathrm{out}|\alpha,\mathrm{in}\rangle.
}
$$

$S$ acts entirely in the asymptotic particle space. It takes the coefficients of an incoming superposition and returns the coefficients with which the same exact state is expanded in the outgoing basis.

If the wave operators are asymptotically complete, both bases cover the same scattering subspace and

$$
S^\dagger S
=
SS^\dagger
=
\mathbf 1.
$$

This is conservation of total probability across <em>all</em> allowed final channels. Later we will turn it into the optical theorem and more detailed constraints on amplitudes.

<Callout title="In and out are not separate Hilbert spaces">
They are two bases associated with two boundary conditions. The overlap between them is nontrivial because the exact dynamics lies between the freely identifiable past and future configurations.
</Callout>

<h2 id="identity-transition">Separate unchanged propagation from interaction</h2>

If the interaction were absent, the in and out dictionaries would agree and $S$ would be the identity. It is therefore useful to write

$$
\boxed{S=\mathbf 1+iT.}
$$

The identity term carries the overlap already present without a collision. The operator $T$ contains genuine transitions generated by the interaction. For identical particles, the identity overlap already includes the required exchange structure; it is not always a single naive Kronecker delta between ordered lists.

For different incoming and outgoing configurations,

$$
S_{\beta\alpha}
=
iT_{\beta\alpha},
$$

while a forward matrix element contains both the unchanged and interacting contributions. Their interference is essential: unitarity relates the imaginary part of forward scattering to the total probability flowing into every possible final state.

<Sidenote>
Here $T$ means the transition operator. We will write the antiunitary time-reversal operator as $\Theta$ so the two ideas never share a symbol.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="invariant-amplitude">Translation symmetry exposes the invariant amplitude</h2>

The asymptotic total four-momentum operator $P_0^\mu$ acts additively on each channel. Translation invariance requires

$$
[P_0^\mu,S]=0,
$$

and therefore also $[P_0^\mu,T]=0$. Take a matrix element between total-momentum eigenstates:

$$
(P_f^\mu-P_i^\mu)
{}_0\langle f|T|i\rangle_0
=
0.
$$

The matrix element can have support only where total four-momentum is conserved. For a fully connected process, define the invariant amplitude $\mathcal M_{fi}$ by

$$
\boxed{
{}_0\langle f|T|i\rangle_{0,\mathrm{conn}}
=
(2\pi)^4
\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)
\mathcal M_{fi}.
}
$$

Equivalently, the connected contribution to $S$ is

$$
{}_0\langle f|S|i\rangle_{0,\mathrm{conn}}
=
i(2\pi)^4
\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)
\mathcal M_{fi},
$$

apart from any identity overlap when the channels coincide.

The two factors have different jobs:

<div className="two-panel-grid">
  <div>
    <strong>$\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)$</strong>
    <p>Records a kinematical selection rule imposed by spacetime translations.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>$\mathcal M_{fi}$</strong>
    <p>Contains the dynamical dependence on energies, angles, particle types, and polarizations.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The numerical factors in the definition of $\mathcal M$ depend on the normalization convention. Throughout this chapter external one-particle states use the covariant normalization

$$
\langle p',\sigma'|p,\sigma\rangle
=
2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3
\delta_{\sigma'\sigma}\delta^3(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p).
$$

Once that ledger is fixed, phase-space and flux formulas can be used consistently. The amplitude is not itself the cross-section.

<Callout title="One overall delta for one connected collision">
Disconnected pieces can contain additional momentum-conservation delta functions. The single $\delta^{(4)}$ above defines a genuinely connected component; extra deltas will acquire a direct physical meaning when we discuss clustering below.
</Callout>

<h2 id="symmetry">The S-matrix is an intertwiner of symmetries</h2>

Suppose an exact symmetry $g$ acts consistently on the incoming and outgoing maps,

$$
U(g)\Omega_{\mathrm{in/out}}
=
\Omega_{\mathrm{in/out}}U_0(g).
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{[S,U_0(g)]=0.}
$$

The scattering operator cannot turn one asymptotic symmetry representation into an incompatible one. This compact statement contains several familiar rules.

<div className="learning-goals">
  <div>
    <strong>Translations</strong>
    <p>Total four-momentum is conserved, producing the overall delta function.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>Lorentz transformations</strong>
    <p>Momenta transform together, while external spin or helicity indices undergo their Wigner transformations.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>Internal symmetries</strong>
    <p>Total conserved charges agree between the incoming and outgoing channels.</p>
  </div>
</div>

For an Abelian charge $Q$,

$$
[Q,S]=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sum_{i\in\mathrm{in}}q_i
=
\sum_{f\in\mathrm{out}}q_f
$$

whenever the amplitude is nonzero. For a non-Abelian symmetry, the total irreducible representation is preserved; Clebsch-Gordan coefficients carry the component dependence and reduced amplitudes contain the dynamics.

Lorentz covariance requires more care than saying “$\mathcal M$ is a scalar.” A spinless amplitude can be a function of Lorentz scalars alone. An amplitude with external spin labels transforms covariantly with the little-group matrices derived in <Link to="/chapters/relativistic-quantum-mechanics/one-particle-states">One-Particle States</Link>. The operator $S$ is invariant; its components depend on the chosen spin or helicity basis.

This also explains why the connected <Link to="/chapters/relativistic-quantum-mechanics/poincare-algebra">Poincaré group</Link> is such a strong organizing principle for scattering. The exact interacting boosts need not equal the free boosts, but the asymptotic $S$-matrix commutes with the free representation carried by detector states.

<h2 id="mandelstam">Two-body kinematics collapses to three invariants</h2>

For a $2\to2$ reaction,

$$
p_1+p_2\longrightarrow p_3+p_4,
$$

define the Mandelstam variables using the $(+,-,-,-)$ metric:

$$
s=(p_1+p_2)^2,
$$

$$
t=(p_1-p_3)^2,
$$

$$
u=(p_1-p_4)^2.
$$

They have immediate geometric meanings:

<div className="learning-goals">
  <div>
    <strong>$s$ channel</strong>
    <p>$s$ is the square of the total center-of-mass energy.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>$t$ channel</strong>
    <p>$t$ measures four-momentum transfer between legs $1$ and $3$.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>$u$ channel</strong>
    <p>$u$ uses the alternative pairing between legs $1$ and $4$.</p>
  </div>
</div>

On shell and with four-momentum conserved,

$$
\boxed{
s+t+u
=
m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2+m_4^2.
}
$$

Only two of the three are independent for fixed external masses. A spinless $2\to2$ invariant amplitude may therefore be written as $\mathcal M(s,t)$, with $u$ fixed by the boxed relation. With spin, one has a family of helicity or spin amplitudes depending on the same kinematic invariants.

The variables do more than abbreviate dot products. Their complex analytic structure will later distinguish thresholds, exchanged channels, and resonance poles.

<h2 id="time-reversal">Time reversal exchanges the two dictionaries</h2>

Time reversal is not an ordinary unitary relabeling. The operator $\Theta$ is antiunitary: it conjugates complex coefficients and reverses momenta and angular momenta. More importantly for scattering, it exchanges the boundary conditions:

$$
\Theta\,\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}\,\Theta^{-1}
=
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}},
\qquad
\Theta\,\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\,\Theta^{-1}
=
\Omega_{\mathrm{in}},
$$

when time reversal is an exact symmetry of the dynamics. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\Theta S\Theta^{-1}
=
S^\dagger.
}
$$

At the level of amplitudes this relates a process to the motion-reversed process: incoming and outgoing roles are exchanged, momenta and spins are reversed, and convention-dependent one-particle phases must be included. Although $\Theta$ is antiunitary, the standard microreversibility relation does not amount to blindly replacing every amplitude by its complex conjugate; the simultaneous exchange of bra and ket matters.

This is also different from parity, which reverses spatial momenta without exchanging past and future. Both symmetries are conditional—real interactions need not respect them. Their one-particle actions are developed in <Link to="/chapters/relativistic-quantum-mechanics/discrete-projective-symmetries">Discrete and Projective Symmetries</Link>.

<h2 id="cluster-decomposition">Distant experiments should become independent</h2>

Consider two collisions, $A$ and $B$, prepared so far apart that no signal or exchanged excitation can connect their interaction regions. Translating laboratory $B$ still farther away should not change the outcomes in laboratory $A$. In that limit the joint scattering amplitudes must factorize:

$$
S_{A+B}
\longrightarrow
S_A\otimes S_B,
$$

with the appropriate exchange sums or signs when identical particles make the two assignments indistinguishable.

This is the <Term>cluster decomposition principle</Term>. It is stronger than total momentum conservation. Translation symmetry allows one overall delta function for the entire combined event; cluster decomposition says that genuinely independent distant experiments behave as independent quantum experiments.

Equivalently, the connected correlation between the two laboratories must fade as their separation grows. Local QFT realizes this principle naturally because interactions are assembled from local densities, though massless fields and infrared dressing can make the manner of the limit more subtle.

<h2 id="connected-components">One momentum delta per connected component</h2>

The full $S$-matrix contains both connected collisions and products of independent subprocesses. Suppose a contribution splits into $k$ nontrivial connected transition components. Translation invariance applies separately to each component, so its distributional structure contains

$$
\boxed{
\prod_{r=1}^{k}
(2\pi)^4
\delta^{(4)}
\!\left(P_{f,r}-P_{i,r}\right).
}
$$

This gives a practical reading rule:

- one connected collision has one overall four-momentum delta function;
- two independent collisions have two delta functions;
- a spectator that passes through unchanged contributes an identity overlap rather than another nontrivial transition amplitude.

For a covariantly normalized spectator, that overlap has the form

$$
2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf p'-\mathbf p).
$$

Its energy is then equal automatically because the external particle remains on the same mass shell. This is why an untouched external line is usually displayed with a three-momentum delta rather than a redundant four-dimensional one.

Extra delta functions are therefore not mysterious extra conservation laws. They reveal that the supposed single process has factorized into separately conserving pieces.

The same organization will later appear diagrammatically. Connected Feynman diagrams contribute to $\mathcal M_{fi}$ after one overall delta function is removed. Disconnected diagrams reconstruct products of lower-point processes, exactly as independent laboratories require.

<Callout title="Connectedness is physical">
A connected diagram is not merely one that happens to be drawn in one piece. It represents the part of an amplitude that cannot be factored into statistically independent scattering experiments after the external particles are divided into groups.
</Callout>

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

The central structure is now:

1. The Møller maps identify exact states by their free particle content in the remote past or future.
2. $S=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}^\dagger\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}$ compares those two identifications.
3. Writing $S=\mathbf 1+iT$ separates unchanged propagation from genuine transition amplitudes.
4. Translation symmetry extracts $\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)$ from each connected component.
5. The remaining invariant amplitude $\mathcal M$ contains dynamics but is not yet an observable rate.
6. Poincaré and internal symmetries constrain which amplitudes can be nonzero and how their spin indices transform.
7. For $2\to2$ scattering, $s$, $t$, and $u$ organize all Lorentz-invariant kinematics.
8. Exact time reversal exchanges the in and out boundary conditions and relates a process to its motion-reversed partner.
9. Cluster decomposition makes widely separated experiments factorize, leaving one momentum delta function per connected subprocess.

The next task is to turn these distribution-valued amplitudes into quantities a detector can count:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal M
+
\text{phase space}
+
\text{incoming flux}
\longrightarrow
\text{rates and cross-sections}.
}
$$
