<h2 id="problem">Problem</h2>

<div className="problem-statement">
  <span className="problem-label">A resonance seen in electron-positron collisions</span>
  <p>
    Imagine a spin-one resonance $R$ centered at a total center-of-mass energy of $150\,\mathrm{GeV}$. At the peak, after averaging over the two incoming spins and summing over the final spins, its elastic $e^+e^-\to R\to e^+e^-$ cross-section is
  </p>

  $$
  \sigma_{ee}^{\rm peak}=10^{-34}\,\mathrm{cm}^2.
  $$

  <p>
    Neglect nonresonant background scattering. Determine the branching fraction $B_e=\Gamma_e/\Gamma$ for $R\to e^+e^-$ and the total $e^+e^-$ cross-section at the peak.
  </p>
</div>

<details className="problem-hint">
  <summary>Hint</summary>
  <p>
    At the peak, the elastic channel contains $B_e^2$: one factor to produce $R$ from $e^+e^-$ and one factor for $R$ to return to $e^+e^-$. The inclusive cross-section contains only the production factor $B_e$.
  </p>
</details>

</p>

<h2 id="formula">1. Start from the resonant cross-section</h2>

For an isolated resonance of spin $J$, the cross-section from a two-body channel $n$ to a channel $n'$ is

$$
\sigma_{n\to n'}(E)
=
\frac{\pi(2J+1)}
{k^2(2s_1+1)(2s_2+1)}
\frac{\Gamma_n\Gamma_{n'}}
{(E-E_R)^2+\Gamma^2/4}.
$$

This formula is independent of whether we write the connected scattering operator as $S=1+i\mathcal T$ or use an alternative sign for the invariant amplitude: the observable contains the absolute square of the pole residue.

For the present channel,

$$
J=1,
\qquad
s_1=s_2=\frac12,
\qquad
n=n'=e^+e^-.
$$

At $E=E_R$, the Breit-Wigner denominator becomes $\Gamma^2/4$. Therefore

$$
\sigma_{ee}^{\rm peak}
=
\frac{\pi\cdot3}{k^2\cdot2\cdot2}
\frac{\Gamma_e^2}{\Gamma^2/4}
=
\boxed{\frac{3\pi}{k^2}B_e^2}.
$$

<Callout title="Why the branching fraction is squared">
The initial $e^+e^-$ state must first couple into the resonance, contributing $\Gamma_e/\Gamma$. An elastic event then asks the resonance to choose $e^+e^-$ again as its final channel, contributing the same fraction a second time.
</Callout>

<h2 id="kinematics">2. Find the center-of-mass momentum</h2>

At total energy $E_R$, each beam particle carries

$$
E_e=\frac{E_R}{2}=75\,\mathrm{GeV}.
$$

The exact momentum is

$$
k
=
\sqrt{\frac{E_R^2}{4}-m_e^2}.
$$

Since $m_e$ is negligible on this scale,

$$
k\simeq75\,\mathrm{GeV}.
$$

The geometric resonant factor is then

$$
\frac{3\pi}{k^2}
=
\frac{3\pi}{(75\,\mathrm{GeV})^2}
=
1.6755\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{GeV}^{-2}.
$$

Use

$$
1\,\mathrm{GeV}^{-2}
=
3.89379\times10^{-28}\,\mathrm{cm}^2.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\frac{3\pi}{k^2}
=
6.5241\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{cm}^2.
}
$$

<h2 id="branching">3. Extract the electronic branching fraction</h2>

Insert the measured elastic peak cross-section:

$$
10^{-34}\,\mathrm{cm}^2
=
\left(6.5241\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{cm}^2\right)B_e^2.
$$

Therefore

$$
B_e
=
\sqrt{
\frac{10^{-34}}
{6.5241\times10^{-31}}
}
=
0.01238.
$$

The branching fraction is

$$
\boxed{
B(R\to e^+e^-)
\simeq1.24\%.
}
$$

The square root is physically important. A very small elastic peak need not mean an equally tiny probability to produce the resonance; elastic detection has already imposed the same small channel twice.

<h2 id="total">4. Sum over every decay channel</h2>

For the inclusive cross-section, sum the final partial widths:

$$
\sum_{n'}\Gamma_{n'}=\Gamma.
$$

At the peak this gives

$$
\sigma_{\rm total}^{\rm peak}
=
\frac{3\pi}{k^2}\frac{\Gamma_e}{\Gamma}
=
\frac{3\pi}{k^2}B_e.
$$

Equivalently, divide the elastic result by the probability that the produced resonance decays back to electrons:

$$
\sigma_{\rm total}^{\rm peak}
=
\frac{\sigma_{ee}^{\rm peak}}{B_e}.
$$

Numerically,

$$
\boxed{
\sigma_{\rm total}^{\rm peak}
=
8.08\times10^{-33}\,\mathrm{cm}^2.
}
$$

<div className="answer-check">
  <strong>Answer check</strong>
  <p>
    The total peak cross-section must exceed the elastic one because it includes every possible decay of the produced resonance. Indeed, $8.08\times10^{-33}\,\mathrm{cm}^2$ is about $80.8$ times the elastic value, exactly $1/B_e$.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 id="interpretation">Interpretation and common traps</h2>

The result illustrates what the peak height can and cannot reveal. With the resonance energy known, the elastic peak fixes $B_e$. It does not separately determine $\Gamma_e$ and $\Gamma$; resolving the line shape or measuring another width-sensitive quantity is needed for that.

Common traps are:

1. Using $B_e$ instead of $B_e^2$ in the elastic channel.
2. Forgetting the average over the four incoming spin states.
3. Treating $\mathrm{GeV}^{-2}$ as though it were already $\mathrm{cm}^2$.
4. Calling every localized enhancement a pure Breit-Wigner peak. Background interference can also turn a resonance into an asymmetric feature or even a dip.

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

For an isolated spin-one resonance formed from unpolarized $e^+e^-$ beams,

$$
\sigma_{ee}^{\rm peak}
=
\frac{3\pi}{k^2}B_e^2,
\qquad
\sigma_{\rm total}^{\rm peak}
=
\frac{3\pi}{k^2}B_e.
$$

The difference between the two formulas is one final-state branching factor.
