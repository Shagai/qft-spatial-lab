<h2 id="standing-wave">1. Define the standing-wave reaction operator</h2>

At fixed energy $E$, write

$$
R(E)
=
\mathcal P\frac{1}{E-H_0}.
$$

The standing-wave equation is

$$
|\Psi_\alpha^0\rangle
=
|\Phi_\alpha\rangle
+R(E_\alpha)V|\Psi_\alpha^0\rangle.
$$

Define the off-shell operator $\mathcal K(E)$ by

$$
\mathcal K(E)|\Phi_\alpha\rangle
=
V|\Psi_\alpha^0\rangle
\qquad(E_\alpha=E).
$$

Multiplying the state equation by $V$ gives

$$
\mathcal K
=
V+VR\mathcal K.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal K
=
(1-VR)^{-1}V
=
V(1-RV)^{-1}.
}
$$

The equality of the two forms follows by expanding either side:

$$
V+VRV+VRVRV+\cdots.
$$

<h2 id="hermiticity">2. Prove Hermiticity</h2>

Because $H_0$ is self-adjoint and the pole is interpreted symmetrically,

$$
R(E)^\dagger=R(E).
$$

The interaction Hamiltonian is also Hermitian:

$$
V^\dagger=V.
$$

Take the adjoint of one form of $\mathcal K$:

$$
\mathcal K^\dagger
=
\left[V(1-RV)^{-1}\right]^\dagger
=
(1-VR)^{-1}V.
$$

But this is the other equivalent form of $\mathcal K$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\mathcal K^\dagger=\mathcal K.}
$$

The source exercise packages the energy-shell delta function into a dimensionless matrix,

$$
K^{\rm src}_{\beta\alpha}
=
\pi\delta(E_\beta-E_\alpha)
\langle\Phi_\beta|\mathcal K(E_\alpha)|\Phi_\alpha\rangle.
$$

The delta function is real and symmetric on shell, so

$$
\boxed{(K^{\rm src})^\dagger=K^{\rm src}.}
$$

<Callout title="Why principal value matters">
The outgoing resolvent is not Hermitian because it contains an on-shell imaginary part. The principal value keeps virtual off-shell propagation but removes that irreversible-looking boundary choice. The standing-wave problem is therefore represented by a Hermitian matrix.
</Callout>

<h2 id="on-shell">3. Restore the on-shell imaginary part</h2>

The outgoing resolvent decomposes as

$$
\frac{1}{E-H_0+i0}
=
R(E)-i\pi\delta(E-H_0).
$$

Let $\tau^+$ be the usual outgoing-wave transition operator,

$$
\tau^+
=
V+V\frac{1}{E-H_0+i0}\tau^+.
$$

Eliminating $V$ in favor of $\mathcal K$ gives

$$
\boxed{
\tau^+
=
\mathcal K
-i\pi\mathcal K\delta(E-H_0)\tau^+.
}
$$

Now package both kernels as dimensionless energy-shell matrices:

$$
A_{\beta\alpha}
=
\pi\delta(E_\beta-E_\alpha)
\tau^+_{\beta\alpha},
$$

$$
K^{\rm src}_{\beta\alpha}
=
\pi\delta(E_\beta-E_\alpha)
\mathcal K_{\beta\alpha}.
$$

The preceding operator equation becomes ordinary matrix algebra on the energy shell:

$$
A
=
K^{\rm src}-iK^{\rm src}A.
$$

Therefore

$$
(1+iK^{\rm src})A=K^{\rm src},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
A
=
(1+iK^{\rm src})^{-1}K^{\rm src}.
}
$$

Because every factor is a function of the same matrix, their order may also be reversed.

<h2 id="cayley">4. Derive the Cayley transform</h2>

In the source's Hamiltonian convention,

$$
S=1-2iA.
$$

Substituting the expression for $A$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
S
&=1-2i(1+iK^{\rm src})^{-1}K^{\rm src}
\\
&=(1-iK^{\rm src})(1+iK^{\rm src})^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the answer in that convention is

$$
\boxed{
S
=
(1-iK^{\rm src})(1+iK^{\rm src})^{-1}.
}
$$

For the notebook, define

$$
K=-K^{\rm src}.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
S
=
(1+iK)(1-iK)^{-1}.
}
$$

Comparing with $S=1+i\mathcal T$ gives

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal T
=
2K(1-iK)^{-1}.
}
$$

The sign change is only a convention translation. It lets the notebook use the familiar elastic relation $K=\tan\delta$.

To check unitarity, note that $K^\dagger=K$ and that $(1+iK)$ commutes with $(1-iK)$. Therefore

$$
S^\dagger
=
(1+iK)^{-1}(1-iK),
$$

so

$$
\boxed{S^\dagger S=1.}
$$

<h2 id="eigenchannel">5. Read the result in one elastic eigenchannel</h2>

Suppose $K$ has a real eigenvalue $\kappa$. The corresponding S-matrix eigenvalue is

$$
S_\kappa
=
\frac{1+i\kappa}{1-i\kappa}.
$$

Every complex number of this form has unit modulus. Set

$$
\kappa=\tan\delta.
$$

Then

$$
S_\kappa
=
\frac{1+i\tan\delta}{1-i\tan\delta}
=
e^{2i\delta}.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{K=\tan\delta}
$$

in each uncoupled elastic channel. The K-matrix is a real coordinate for motion around the unitary S-matrix circle.

  <strong>Answer check</strong>
  <p>
    If $K=0$, then $S=1$. If $K$ is Hermitian, every eigenvalue $\kappa$ is real and $(1+i\kappa)/(1-i\kappa)$ lies on the unit circle. The construction therefore has the correct no-scattering limit and enforces unitarity eigenchannel by eigenchannel.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 id="interpretation">Interpretation and common traps</h2>

The K-matrix separates two ideas that are entangled in the usual outgoing resolvent. The principal-value part describes dispersive virtual propagation; the explicit on-shell delta function supplies the absorptive contribution demanded by open channels. The Cayley transform recombines them without losing unitarity.

Common traps are:

1. Claiming $VR$ is Hermitian. It generally is not; Hermiticity follows only after resumming the complete operator and using both equivalent forms.
2. Dropping the energy-shell delta function when moving from $\mathcal K$ to the dimensionless matrix $K$.
3. Mixing the source sign with the project's $S=1+i\mathcal T$ sign. The two Cayley transforms look inverted until $K=-K^{\rm src}$ is stated.
4. Assuming a truncated ordinary Born series is exactly unitary. A Hermitian K-matrix parametrization is useful precisely because its Cayley transform remains unitary after a controlled approximation to $K$.

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

The logical chain is

$$
\text{principal-value resolvent}
\Longrightarrow
K^\dagger=K
\Longrightarrow
S=(1+iK)(1-iK)^{-1}
\Longrightarrow
S^\dagger S=1.
$$

For one elastic channel, this reduces to $K=\tan\delta$ and $S=e^{2i\delta}$.
