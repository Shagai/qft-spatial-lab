<h2 id="question">How does an amplitude become something a detector counts?</h2>

The scattering matrix answers a quantum question:

$$
S_{fi}
=
\langle f,\mathrm{out}|i,\mathrm{in}\rangle.
$$

An experiment reports a different kind of object. A decay experiment measures a lifetime or a branching fraction. A collider reports an event rate or a cross-section. Neither is obtained by writing $|S_{fi}|^2$ and stopping.

Three pieces have to be combined:

$$
\boxed{
\text{dynamics}
\times
\text{available final states}
\div
\text{initial exposure}
}
$$

The invariant amplitude $\mathcal M_{fi}$ contains the dynamics. Lorentz-invariant phase space counts the final states. Time supplies the exposure for a decay, while incoming flux supplies it for a collision.

<ScatteringFlowFigure />

The virtue of this organization is that the same amplitude can be used in any frame. All frame-dependent bookkeeping is carried by explicitly kinematical factors.

<h2 id="conservation-delta">First remove the universal conservation law</h2>

Use covariantly normalized one-particle momentum states,

$$
\langle q,r|p,s\rangle
=
2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3
\delta_{rs}\delta^3(\mathbf q-\mathbf p).
$$

For a genuine transition, the nontrivial part of the $S$-matrix has the form

$$
\boxed{
\langle f|S|i\rangle_{\mathrm{connected}}
=
i(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)\mathcal M_{fi}.
}
$$

The four-dimensional delta function is not part of the interaction dynamics. It says that every allowed event conserves the total four-momentum. The invariant amplitude is what remains after that universal statement has been factored out.

Different sign conventions may place $-i$ instead of $+i$ in this definition. Rates depend on $|\mathcal M|^2$, so the convention is harmless as long as it is used consistently.

<Callout title="Connected means that something happened">
The full $S$-matrix also contains the identity contribution and terms in which some particles pass through as unchanged spectators. A connected amplitude isolates the part in which the listed external particles participate in one linked process. Disconnected experiments reappear later through products of connected pieces.
</Callout>

<h2 id="delta-squared">Why a squared delta function is a warning, not a disaster</h2>

Squaring the plane-wave matrix element seems to produce

$$
\left[\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)\right]^2.
$$

That expression is telling us that an ideal momentum eigenstate fills all space and exists for all time. It is not a normalizable laboratory preparation. The apparent infinity comes from asking an infinitely extended beam to interact for an infinite duration.

Real particles arrive in <Term>wave packets</Term>. They overlap only in a finite region and during a finite time. One can make this precise with packets from the beginning. A quicker regulator puts the system in a large spatial volume $V$ and observes it for a time $T$. Schematically,

$$
\delta^3(\mathbf 0)\longleftrightarrow \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3},
\qquad
\delta(0)\longleftrightarrow \frac{T}{2\pi}.
$$

One conservation delta remains in the final answer. The other copy becomes the volume-time exposure. Dividing by $T$ produces a rate; dividing by the incident density and relative flux produces a cross-section. The limits $V,T\to\infty$ are taken only after those physical quantities have been formed.

<Sidenote>
The packet derivation is conceptually cleaner because it never squares a distribution. The box-and-time argument is useful bookkeeping, but $V$ and $T$ are regulators rather than new physical parameters of the collision.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="phase-space">Invariant phase space counts the allowed outcomes</h2>

For one positive-energy on-shell particle,

$$
d\Pi(p)
=
\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_{\mathbf p}}
$$

is Lorentz invariant. For an $n$-particle final state with total incoming momentum $P$, define

$$
\boxed{
d\Phi_n(P;p_1,\ldots,p_n)
=
(2\pi)^4
\delta^{(4)}\!\left(P-\sum_{a=1}^{n}p_a\right)
\prod_{a=1}^{n}
\frac{d^3\mathbf p_a}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_a}.
}
$$

Each factor lets one final particle range over its mass shell. The delta function then keeps only combinations whose total energy and momentum match the initial state.

This separates two questions:

| Question | Encoded by |
| --- | --- |
| Which final momenta are kinematically available? | $d\Phi_n$ |
| How strongly does the interaction favor each configuration? | $|\mathcal M_{fi}|^2$ |

A large phase-space region does not guarantee a large rate: the amplitude may suppress it. A large amplitude cannot create a forbidden final state: phase space vanishes outside the kinematically allowed region.

<h2 id="decays">A decay width is probability per proper time</h2>

Let one particle of mass $M$ decay into $n$ particles. In its rest frame,

$$
\boxed{
d\Gamma
=
\frac{1}{2M}
\overline{|\mathcal M_{i\to f}|^2}
d\Phi_n.
}
$$

Integrating over all allowed final configurations gives the partial width for that channel. Summing over mutually exclusive channels gives the total width,

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
\sum_X\Gamma(i\to X).
$$

In natural units,

$$
\tau=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}},
\qquad
\operatorname{Br}(i\to X)
=
\frac{\Gamma(i\to X)}{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}}.
$$

$\Gamma$ is normally the rest-frame width. If the parent has energy $E$ in the laboratory, time dilation gives

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{lab}}
=
\frac{M}{E}\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{rest}}.
$$

The intrinsic width is unchanged; the decay clock advances more slowly relative to laboratory time.

<h2 id="collisions">A cross-section is a transition rate divided by incoming flux</h2>

For two incoming particles, define the invariant flux factor

$$
F
=
4\sqrt{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
d\sigma(1+2\to f)
=
\frac{1}{F}
\overline{|\mathcal M_{1 2\to f}|^2}
d\Phi_n.
}
$$

In the center-of-momentum frame,

$$
F=4\sqrt{s}\,|\mathbf p_i|,
\qquad
s=(p_1+p_2)^2.
$$

If particle 1 is at rest, this becomes $F=4m_1E_2|\mathbf v_2|$. These are the expected density-times-relative-motion factors written in a form every inertial observer can use.

Sometimes the same flux is expressed through the Møller quantity

$$
v_{\mathrm M}
=
\frac{\sqrt{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}}{E_1E_2},
\qquad
F=4E_1E_2v_{\mathrm M}.
$$

<Callout title="The Møller quantity is not a signal speed">
In some frames $v_{\mathrm M}$ can be larger than one. Nothing travels faster than light: this quantity packages the invariant beam flux and is not the velocity of one physical object as measured in the rest frame of another.
</Callout>

<h2 id="spin-counting">Say explicitly what the bar over the amplitude means</h2>

For unpolarized beams and unobserved final polarizations,

$$
\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}
=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{initial}}}
\sum_{\substack{\text{initial spins or colors}\\
\text{final spins or colors}}}
|\mathcal M|^2.
$$

The initial labels are averaged because the preparation is a statistical mixture. The unmeasured final labels are summed because any one of them counts as the event. For polarized particles, do not average over the polarization that was deliberately prepared.

Identical final particles require another piece of bookkeeping. If $n_a$ indistinguishable particles of species $a$ are integrated over the full labeled phase space, include

$$
\frac{1}{S_f},
\qquad
S_f=\prod_a n_a!.
$$

Equivalently, integrate each physical configuration only once. Doing both would undercount the events.

<h2 id="two-body">Two-body kinematics collapses to one momentum magnitude</h2>

For

$$
1+2\longrightarrow3+4,
$$

work in the center-of-momentum frame. The initial and final momenta have the form

$$
p_1=(E_1,\mathbf p_i),
\qquad
p_2=(E_2,-\mathbf p_i),
$$

$$
p_3=(E_3,\mathbf p_f),
\qquad
p_4=(E_4,-\mathbf p_f).
$$

Introduce the Källén function

$$
\lambda(x,y,z)
=
x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz.
$$

Energy-momentum conservation fixes

$$
|\mathbf p_i|
=
\frac{\sqrt{\lambda(s,m_1^2,m_2^2)}}{2\sqrt{s}},
\qquad
|\mathbf p_f|
=
\frac{\sqrt{\lambda(s,m_3^2,m_4^2)}}{2\sqrt{s}}.
$$

It also fixes the final energies, for example

$$
E_3
=
\frac{s+m_3^2-m_4^2}{2\sqrt{s}}.
$$

Only the direction of $\mathbf p_f$ remains free. The two-body phase-space measure reduces to

$$
d\Phi_2
=
\frac{1}{16\pi^2}
\frac{|\mathbf p_f|}{\sqrt{s}}\,d\Omega,
$$

and the differential cross-section becomes

$$
\boxed{
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{1}{64\pi^2s}
\frac{|\mathbf p_f|}{|\mathbf p_i|}
\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}.
}
$$

If particles 3 and 4 are identical and the full sphere is integrated, the appropriate $1/2!$ factor still has to be included.

For a two-body decay $A\to1+2$ at rest,

$$
\frac{d\Gamma}{d\Omega}
=
\frac{|\mathbf p|}{32\pi^2M_A^2}
\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}.
$$

If the squared amplitude is angle independent, integration gives

$$
\Gamma
=
\frac{|\mathbf p|}{8\pi M_A^2}
\overline{|\mathcal M|^2},
$$

again before any identical-particle factor.

<h2 id="thresholds">Kinematics draws the boundary; dynamics draws the pattern</h2>

The final momentum is real only when

$$
\lambda(s,m_3^2,m_4^2)\geq0.
$$

For positive-energy final particles this gives the production threshold

$$
\sqrt{s}\geq m_3+m_4.
$$

Near threshold, $|\mathbf p_f|$ shrinks and phase space suppresses the rate even before any dynamical suppression is considered. Angular momentum can add further powers of momentum: higher partial waves have more difficulty emerging when little kinetic energy is available.

Above threshold, the angular distribution probes the amplitude. In the center-of-momentum frame,

$$
t
=
m_1^2+m_3^2-2E_1E_3
+2|\mathbf p_i||\mathbf p_f|\cos\theta.
$$

Thus $d\sigma/d\Omega$ may equally be viewed as information about how $\mathcal M(s,t)$ varies across the allowed range of $t$. A flat phase-space factor does not imply a flat angular distribution; exchanged particles, spin, and interference live in the amplitude.

<h2 id="dalitz">Three-body decays expose dynamics across a region</h2>

A three-body final state has more freedom after total four-momentum is conserved. For a decay $A\to1+2+3$, useful invariant coordinates are

$$
s_{12}=(p_1+p_2)^2,
\qquad
s_{23}=(p_2+p_3)^2.
$$

The third pair invariant is not independent because

$$
s_{12}+s_{23}+s_{13}
=
M_A^2+m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2.
$$

Plotting events in the allowed $(s_{12},s_{23})$ region gives a <Term>Dalitz plot</Term>. If the amplitude is approximately constant, the invariant event density provides a simple phase-space baseline. A narrow band indicates that one particle pair repeatedly forms an intermediate resonance. Crossed bands and nonuniform patches reveal interference and spin-dependent structure.

The plot therefore separates two kinds of information visually: its outer boundary is kinematics, while structure inside the boundary is dynamics.

<h2 id="event-counts">The final step is experimental exposure</h2>

For colliding beams, the machine supplies a luminosity $\mathcal L$. The expected event rate in a channel is

$$
\frac{dN}{dt}
=
\mathcal L\,\sigma.
$$

After integrating over a run,

$$
N_{\mathrm{expected}}
=
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}
\,\sigma
\,\operatorname{Br}
\,\varepsilon,
$$

where $\operatorname{Br}$ accounts for any selected decay chain and $\varepsilon$ summarizes acceptance and detection efficiency. A differential prediction is treated in the same way bin by bin.

The complete bridge is now visible:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal M
\longrightarrow
|\mathcal M|^2d\Phi
\longrightarrow
\Gamma\ \text{or}\ \sigma
\longrightarrow
\text{events in a detector}.
}
$$

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

The essential ideas are:

1. The conservation delta function is universal kinematics; $\mathcal M$ is the interaction-dependent amplitude.
2. Squared delta functions arise from ideal plane waves and become finite exposure factors when wave packets or regulators are used correctly.
3. Lorentz-invariant phase space counts on-shell final configurations while enforcing total four-momentum conservation.
4. A decay width divides transition probability by elapsed proper time; a cross-section divides the collision rate by incoming flux.
5. Initial unresolved labels are averaged, final unresolved labels are summed, and identical configurations are counted only once.
6. The Källén function fixes two-body momenta and makes thresholds transparent.
7. Event distributions compare the kinematical phase-space baseline with the dynamical pattern in $|\mathcal M|^2$.
