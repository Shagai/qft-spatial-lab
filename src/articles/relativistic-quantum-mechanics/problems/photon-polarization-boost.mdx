<h2 id="problem">Problem</h2>

<div className="problem-statement">
  <span className="problem-label">Linear polarization under a transverse observer boost</span>
  <p>
    Observer $O$ sees a photon moving along $+y$ with linear polarization along $z$. Observer $O'$ moves with velocity $v$ along $+z$ relative to $O$. What momentum, propagation direction, and physical polarization does $O'$ report?
  </p>
</div>

<details className="problem-hint">
  <summary>Hint</summary>
  <p>
    Boost both the null momentum $k^\mu$ and a polarization representative $\epsilon^\mu$. If the transformed polarization has a nonzero time component, use $\epsilon^\mu\sim\epsilon^\mu+\alpha k^\mu$ before interpreting its spatial direction.
  </p>
</details>

<p>
  The representation-theory reason for the gauge step is explained in <Link to="/chapters/relativistic-quantum-mechanics/one-particle-states#photon">the photon-polarization section</Link>: null rotations change a four-vector representative without changing the physical helicity state.
</p>

<Callout title="What counts as the same polarization">
For a photon, $\epsilon^\mu$ and $\epsilon^\mu+\alpha k^\mu$ describe the same physical state. The field strength and every amplitude coupled to a conserved current are unchanged.
</Callout>

<h2 id="boost">1. Boost the momentum and polarization</h2>

Use the initial representatives

$$
k^\mu=(p,0,p,0),
\qquad
\epsilon^\mu=(0,0,0,1).
$$

They satisfy

$$
k^2=0,
\qquad
k\cdot\epsilon=0,
\qquad
\epsilon^2=-1.
$$

Because $O'$ moves along $+z$, apply $B_z(-\eta)$ with $\tanh\eta=v$ and $\gamma_v=(1-v^2)^{-1/2}$. The momentum becomes

$$
\boxed{
k'^\mu
=
(\gamma_vp,\,0,\,p,\,-\gamma_vvp).
}
$$

Its energy is $\gamma_vp$. This is the transverse Doppler shift: the initial photon direction is perpendicular to the observer velocity, but it is not perpendicular after the frame change.

The polarization representative becomes

$$
\widetilde\epsilon'^\mu
=
B_z(-\eta)\epsilon^\mu
=
(-\gamma_vv,\,0,\,0,\,\gamma_v).
$$

The time component is not an error. Direct substitution confirms

$$
k'\cdot\widetilde\epsilon'=0,
\qquad
\widetilde\epsilon'^2=-1.
$$

So the boosted vector is still a valid covariant polarization representative. It is merely inconvenient for reading the electric-field direction.

<h2 id="gauge">2. Choose a purely spatial representative</h2>

Use the gauge equivalence

$$
\epsilon'^\mu
=
\widetilde\epsilon'^\mu+\alpha k'^\mu.
$$

To set $\epsilon'^0=0$, solve

$$
-\gamma_vv+\alpha\gamma_vp=0,
$$

which gives

$$
\alpha=\frac vp.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon'^\mu
&=
(-\gamma_vv,0,0,\gamma_v)
+\frac vp(\gamma_vp,0,p,-\gamma_vvp)\\
&=
\boxed{(0,\,0,\,v,\,\gamma_v^{-1})}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The physical spatial polarization is consequently

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf e'=(0,v,\gamma_v^{-1}).
}
$$

It has unit length:

$$
|\mathbf e'|^2
=v^2+\gamma_v^{-2}
=v^2+(1-v^2)
=1.
$$

<Sidenote>
The gauge shift changed the representative, not the photon. In helicity language, a proper Lorentz transformation preserves the $+1$ and $-1$ sectors; their relative phase changes the direction of linear polarization.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="geometry">3. Read the aberrated geometry</h2>

Divide the spatial momentum by the new photon energy:

$$
\widehat{\mathbf k}'
=
\frac{\mathbf k'}{k'^0}
=
\boxed{(0,\gamma_v^{-1},-v)}.
$$

The physical transversality check is now ordinary Euclidean orthogonality:

$$
\widehat{\mathbf k}'\cdot\mathbf e'
=
\frac v{\gamma_v}-\frac v{\gamma_v}
=0.
$$

Define an angle $\alpha$ by

$$
\sin\alpha=v,
\qquad
\cos\alpha=\gamma_v^{-1}.
$$

Then

$$
\widehat{\mathbf k}'=(0,\cos\alpha,-\sin\alpha),
$$

$$
\mathbf e'=(0,\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha).
$$

The propagation-polarization frame has rotated rigidly through $-\alpha$ about $x$. The photon remains transverse throughout; only the observer's decomposition into space and time has changed.

  <strong>Answer check</strong>
  <p>
    We have $k'^2=0$, $\epsilon'^2=-1$, $k'\cdot\epsilon'=0$, and $\epsilon'^0=0$. These four checks establish that the final representative is null-compatible, normalized, transverse, and directly interpretable as a spatial polarization.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 id="interpretation">Interpretation and common traps</h2>

There are two equivalent descriptions:

1. Boost the polarization four-vector and retain its time component. This keeps Lorentz covariance manifest.
2. Add a multiple of $k'^\mu$ and use a purely spatial unit polarization. This makes the detector interpretation manifest.

They are not two different photon states.

**Common trap:** interpreting $\widetilde\epsilon'^0\neq0$ as a longitudinal photon. A massive vector has a physical longitudinal spin state, but a photon does not acquire one under a change of frame. The unwanted component lies exactly along the gauge-redundant direction.

Another trap is boosting only the spatial vector $(0,0,1)$. Polarization belongs in spacetime before a gauge is chosen; treating it as an ordinary Euclidean arrow hides the compensating gauge transformation.

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

Observer $O'$ reports

$$
\boxed{
k'^\mu=(\gamma_vp,0,p,-\gamma_vvp),
}
$$

with propagation and polarization directions

$$
\boxed{
\widehat{\mathbf k}'=(0,\gamma_v^{-1},-v),
\qquad
\mathbf e'=(0,v,\gamma_v^{-1}).
}
$$

The boost aberrates both directions while gauge freedom keeps the physical polarization purely transverse.
