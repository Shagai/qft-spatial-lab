<h2 id="little-group">1. Find the null little group</h2>

Work with

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(+,-,-)
$$

and choose a standard future-directed null momentum along $+y$:

$$
k^\mu=(\kappa,0,\kappa),
\qquad
k^2=0.
$$

Let

$$
J=M^{12}
$$

generate rotations in the spatial plane and let

$$
K_x=M^{10}
$$

generate boosts along $x$. With the notebook commutator conventions, the combination

$$
\boxed{N=J-K_x}
$$

preserves $k$. We can verify this without writing a finite matrix. The relevant commutators are

$$
[J,P_x]=iP_y,
\qquad
[J,P_y]=-iP_x,
$$

$$
[K_x,H]=iP_x,
\qquad
[K_x,P_x]=iH,
\qquad
[K_x,P_y]=0.
$$

Thus

$$
[N,H]=-iP_x,
$$

$$
[N,P_x]=i(P_y-H),
$$

$$
[N,P_y]=-iP_x.
$$

On the state with $H=P_y=\kappa$ and $P_x=0$, every right-hand side vanishes. Acting with $N$ therefore keeps all momentum eigenvalues fixed.

There is only one continuous little-group generator. Its finite transformations satisfy

$$
N(a)=e^{-iaN},
$$

$$
N(a)N(b)=N(a+b).
$$

The connected massless little group is

$$
\boxed{(\mathbb R,+).}
$$

It is a line of null rotations, not a circle of ordinary spatial rotations.

<h2 id="representations">2. Represent the little group</h2>

Every irreducible unitary representation of the additive group $\mathbb R$ is a one-dimensional character:

$$
\boxed{
U(N(a))|k,\lambda\rangle
=e^{-ia\lambda}|k,\lambda\rangle,
\qquad
\lambda\in\mathbb R.
}
$$

Choose standard transformations $L(p)$ with $L(p)k=p$. For a general proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation,

$$
W(\Lambda,p)
=
L^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda L(p)
=N(a(\Lambda,p)).
$$

The one-particle state transforms as

$$
\boxed{
U(\Lambda)|p,\lambda\rangle
=e^{-ia(\Lambda,p)\lambda}
|\Lambda p,\lambda\rangle.
}
$$

There is one state per momentum for a fixed character. The label is not quantized because the group parameter never returns to the identity after a finite nonzero period.

This already shows why “helicity” is the wrong word. Helicity comes from a compact rotation around the ray. Here the momentum-preserving operation is a noncompact mixture of a rotation and a boost.

<h2 id="invariant">3. Package the label invariantly</h2>

In (2+1) dimensions the Pauli-Lubanski object is the pseudoscalar

$$
W
=
\frac12\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}
P^\mu M^{\nu\rho}.
$$

For the chosen momentum,

$$
W=\kappa(J-K_x)=\kappa N.
$$

It is therefore better to label the representation by the invariant eigenvalue $\rho$:

$$
\boxed{
W|p,\rho\rangle=\rho|p,\rho\rangle,
\qquad
\rho=\kappa\lambda.
}
$$

The scale $\kappa$ was arbitrary, while $\rho$ is a Casimir eigenvalue and does not depend on the chosen reference ray.

Two branches are possible:

<div className="two-panel-grid">
  <div>
    <strong>Trivial null rotation: $\rho=0$</strong>
    <p>The little group acts trivially. Familiar massless finite-component fields in (2+1) dimensions realize this branch.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>Nontrivial null rotation: $\rho\neq0$</strong>
    <p>The state acquires a genuine momentum-preserving phase. This is the lower-dimensional analogue of a continuous-spin representation.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Representation theory allows the second branch even though familiar particle models generally use the first. Symmetry classifies possibilities; it does not promise that nature realizes every one.

<Sidenote>
The nonzero branch is sometimes called a massless anyonic or “panyon” representation. The useful statement here is the group-theoretic one: it is a nontrivial character of the null little group, labeled invariantly by $\rho$.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="inversions">4. Extend to the full (O(2,1)) group</h2>

Reflect the transverse coordinate $x$:

$$
\mathsf P_x:(t,x,y)\mapsto(t,-x,y).
$$

This reflection leaves the reference momentum $k=(\kappa,0,\kappa)$ fixed, but it reverses the little-group generator:

$$
\mathsf P_xN\mathsf P_x^{-1}=-N.
$$

Equivalently, because $W$ is a pseudoscalar,

$$
\mathsf P_xW\mathsf P_x^{-1}=-W.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathsf P_x:\rho\longmapsto-\rho.}
$$

Time reversal also reverses the Pauli-Lubanski pseudoscalar, after its antiunitary action and reversal of spatial momentum are taken into account:

$$
\boxed{\mathsf T:\rho\longmapsto-\rho.}
$$

For $\rho\neq0$, a single character of the connected little group cannot be extended by itself to the full group. The minimal inversion-closed multiplet is

$$
\boxed{
|p,+\rho\rangle,
\qquad
|p,-\rho\rangle,
}
$$

with parity or time reversal exchanging the two.

For $\rho=0$, the connected little group acts trivially, so an inversion may keep the single sector fixed. A reflection can then act by an intrinsic sign or phase consistent with its group relations.

<h2 id="interpretation">Interpretation and common traps</h2>

<div className="answer-check">
  <strong>Dimensional check</strong>
  <p>
    In (d) spacetime dimensions the familiar massless little group has Euclidean form $ISO(d-2)$. Setting $d=3$ leaves $ISO(1)$: its connected component is precisely the translation group $\mathbb R$, with no continuous transverse rotation.
  </p>
</div>

The massive and massless cases in (2+1) dimensions should not be conflated:

- massive spin is a phase under the compact rest-frame rotation $SO(2)$;
- massless $\rho$ is a phase under a noncompact null rotation $\mathbb R$.

Both labels can be real, but for different geometric reasons.

**Common trap:** setting the little-group action to zero simply because there is no helicity. The trivial branch $\rho=0$ is familiar, but a nontrivial one-dimensional character is mathematically allowed and must be included in a complete classification.

Another trap is forgetting that the problem asks about $O(2,1)$ rather than only its identity component. The disconnected reflection sends $\rho$ to $-\rho$, so nonzero sectors come in pairs when inversion symmetry is imposed.

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

For a massless particle in (2+1) dimensions, the connected little group is

$$
\boxed{\mathbb R,}
$$

and its states may carry a real Pauli-Lubanski eigenvalue

$$
\boxed{W|p,\rho\rangle=\rho|p,\rho\rangle.}
$$

$\rho=0$ gives the familiar trivial action; $\rho\neq0$ gives a nontrivial null-rotation phase. Extending to parity or time reversal pairs the nonzero sectors:

$$
\boxed{+\rho\longleftrightarrow-\rho.}
$$

There is no ordinary helicity because there is no transverse rotation plane.
