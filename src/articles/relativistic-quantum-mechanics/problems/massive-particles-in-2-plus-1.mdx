<h2 id="problem">Problem</h2>

<div className="problem-statement">
  <span className="problem-label">A massive particle in one fewer spatial dimension</span>
  <p>
    Suppose spacetime has two spatial directions and one time direction, with connected Lorentz symmetry $SO^+(2,1)$. How should the spin states of one massive particle be described? How do they transform under Lorentz transformations, and what changes when parity or time reversal is included?
  </p>
</div>

<details className="problem-hint">
  <summary>Hint</summary>
  <p>
    Put the particle at rest and ask which Lorentz transformations leave $k^\mu=(m,0,0)$ fixed. There is only one spatial rotation plane, so the little group is Abelian.
  </p>
</details>

<p>
  The method is exactly the one developed in <Link to="/chapters/relativistic-quantum-mechanics/one-particle-states#little-group">the little-group construction</Link>. Only the spacetime dimension—and therefore the group that survives at fixed momentum—has changed.
</p>

<Callout title="A genuine parity in two spatial dimensions">
The map $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$ is just a rotation by $\pi$, so it belongs to the connected rotation group. A disconnected parity transformation must reflect one coordinate, for example $(t,x,y)\mapsto(t,-x,y)$.
</Callout>

<h2 id="little-group">1. Find the massive little group</h2>

Use coordinates

$$
x^\mu=(t,x,y),
\qquad
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(+,-,-).
$$

For a massive positive-energy particle choose

$$
k^\mu=(m,0,0).
$$

A boost changes the zero spatial momentum, so no nontrivial boost preserves $k$. A rotation in the $xy$ plane does preserve it. The little group is therefore

$$
\boxed{SO(2).}
$$

There is only one rotation generator,

$$
J=M^{12}.
$$

Because $SO(2)$ is Abelian, every irreducible unitary representation is one-dimensional. Choose a rest state satisfying

$$
J|k,s\rangle=s|k,s\rangle.
$$

A rotation through angle $\theta$ acts by the phase

$$
\boxed{
U(R(\theta))|k,s\rangle
=e^{-is\theta}|k,s\rangle.
}
$$

The number $s$ is the spin. Unlike in three spatial dimensions, it is not one component inside a larger multiplet.

<h2 id="states">2. Induce the rest state over the mass shell</h2>

For each momentum $p$ choose a standard Lorentz transformation satisfying

$$
L(p)k=p,
$$

and define

$$
|p,s\rangle=U(L(p))|k,s\rangle.
$$

Given any proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation $\Lambda$, compare the actual path with the standard path:

$$
W(\Lambda,p)
=
L^{-1}(\Lambda p)\Lambda L(p).
$$

This transformation leaves $k$ fixed, so it is a planar rotation,

$$
W(\Lambda,p)=R(\theta(\Lambda,p)).
$$

With covariant normalization,

$$
\boxed{
U(\Lambda)|p,s\rangle
=
e^{-is\theta(\Lambda,p)}
|\Lambda p,s\rangle.
}
$$

There is one state for each momentum in a fixed spin sector. The Lorentz transformation changes the momentum and rephases the state; there is no sum over $2s+1$ components.

The (2+1)-dimensional Pauli-Lubanski object is a pseudoscalar rather than a four-vector:

$$
W
=
\frac12\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}
P^\mu M^{\nu\rho}.
$$

At rest,

$$
W=mJ,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{W|p,s\rangle=ms|p,s\rangle.}
$$

This makes $s=W/m$ invariant even though the phase $\theta(\Lambda,p)$ depends on the chosen standard boosts.

<h2 id="topology">3. Why the spin need not be integral</h2>

If one insists on a single-valued representation of the ordinary circle group $SO(2)$, then

$$
R(\theta+2\pi)=R(\theta)
$$

requires

$$
e^{-2\pi i s}=1,
$$

and hence $s\in\mathbb Z$.

Quantum states, however, are rays. Projective representations of $SO^+(2,1)$ are ordinary representations of an appropriate covering group. The universal cover unwraps the rotation circle:

$$
\theta\in\mathbb R
$$

with no requirement that a $2\pi$ path act as the identity on the state vector. It therefore permits

$$
\boxed{s\in\mathbb R.}
$$

The double cover is the familiar intermediate choice that allows integer and half-integer spin. The universal cover permits fractional spin, which in interacting (2+1)-dimensional quantum systems is tied to braid statistics and anyons.

<Sidenote>
The statement is kinematical: symmetry permits real $s$. Whether a particular local or emergent theory contains a particle with that spin is a dynamical question.
</Sidenote>

<h2 id="inversions">4. Add parity and time reversal</h2>

Choose parity to reflect one coordinate:

$$
\mathsf P_x:(t,x,y)\mapsto(t,-x,y).
$$

This reverses the orientation of the spatial plane, so

$$
\mathsf P_xJ\mathsf P_x^{-1}=-J.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\mathsf P_xW\mathsf P_x^{-1}=-W,
$$

and parity maps

$$
\boxed{s\longmapsto-s.}
$$

Schematically,

$$
\mathsf P_x|p,s\rangle
=
\eta_P|\mathsf P_xp,-s\rangle.
$$

Time reversal is antiunitary and reverses spatial momentum and angular momentum. It also sends

$$
W\longmapsto-W,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
\mathsf T|p,s\rangle
=
\eta_T|\mathsf Tp,-s\rangle,
}
$$

with complex coefficients conjugated by the antiunitary action.

Thus a nonzero-$s$ irreducible sector of the connected group cannot be closed under either inversion by itself. If parity or time reversal is an exact symmetry, the spectrum must also contain a degenerate partner with spin $-s$.

For $s=0$, a single sector can map to itself, up to an intrinsic parity phase and the usual possibilities for the square of time reversal.

<h2 id="interpretation">Interpretation and common traps</h2>

<div className="answer-check">
  <strong>Group-theory check</strong>
  <p>
    The rest little group has one continuous generator and its irreducible unitary representations have dimension one. The answer must therefore contain one phase label—not the three states that a spin-one particle would have in (3+1) dimensions.
  </p>
</div>

The word “spin” is doing the same job as before: it says how a state responds to transformations that preserve a reference momentum. What changes is the little group. In (3+1) dimensions it is $SO(3)$ for a massive particle; here it is $SO(2)$.

**Common trap:** writing $\sigma=-s,-s+1,\ldots,s$. That list belongs to an $SU(2)$ representation and has no place in an irreducible $SO(2)$ sector.

Another trap is defining parity by flipping both spatial coordinates. That operation is a connected rotation by $\pi$ and therefore does not test the orientation-reversing symmetry that pairs $s$ with $-s$.

<h2 id="checkpoint">Checkpoint</h2>

For a massive particle in (2+1) dimensions,

$$
\boxed{
U(\Lambda)|p,s\rangle
=
e^{-is\theta(\Lambda,p)}|\Lambda p,s\rangle,
\qquad
s\in\mathbb R
}
$$

when projective representations, or the universal cover, are allowed. A fixed $s$ gives one state per momentum. Parity and time reversal reverse the spin:

$$
\boxed{s\leftrightarrow-s.}
$$

An inversion-symmetric theory therefore pairs every nonzero-spin species with its opposite-spin partner.
